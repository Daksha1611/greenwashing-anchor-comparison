## Research question

Have Australian companies changed how they talk about their carbon footprints? And when they
legitimise themselves, do they lean on **symbolism** (looking responsible) or on **substantive
action** (behaving responsibly)?

## Method

**Content analysis** (a human coder reads and classifies text) of the sustainability and annual
reports of the ASX Top 50 companies, comparing **2008 with 2005**. For each report the author
records how much is said about carbon footprints, whether each item is **action** or **symbolism**,
and whether **attention-attracting devices** are used. Results are reported separately for the
**more carbon-intensive** and the **less carbon-intensive** sectors.

## Headline results

From the abstract:

- Footprint-related disclosure rates are **increasing**, and disclosure is being **signalled more
  prominently**.
- **Carbon-intensive sectors** appear to pursue a **moral legitimation strategy underpinned by
  substantive action**.
- **Less carbon-intensive sectors** rely **more heavily on symbolic disclosure**.

## Reality anchor

**Sector carbon intensity**, a coarse stand-in for emissions. There is no firm-level emissions
figure; the comparison is between groups of sectors.

## How it relates to our project

- **An early manual version of the symbolic/substantive split.** Paper 4 (Wang et al.) automates
  essentially the same distinction with a language model, and note 07's cheap-talk index is a
  further automated version. Hrasky shows the idea predates the NLP methods.
- **Sector matters, and in a direction one might not guess.** The symbolic talk sits in the
  *low-emission* sectors, not the heavy emitters. This is a reason to include sector fixed effects
  and to check whether our talk score behaves differently by sector. A pooled comparison could hide
  opposite patterns inside different sectors.

## Limitations

(From the abstract's own caveats and our reading.)

- **Small sample of large firms:** the ASX Top 50 only, so no claim about smaller firms or other
  countries.
- Two time points (2005 and 2008) and a coarse sector split, not firm-level emissions.
- Based on the abstract only.
