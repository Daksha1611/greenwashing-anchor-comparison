## Research question

Three questions, from the abstract:

1. Does recent CSR reporting differ from the CSR reporting of the **1970s**?
2. Does a major finding of older CSR research still hold: that disclosure depends largely on
   **exposure to legitimacy factors** (a firm's size, industry and visibility)?
3. Do investors actually **value** CSR disclosure, as newer mainstream studies argue?

## Method

Fortune 500 data from the **late 1970s** (an Ernst & Ernst 1978 survey) and a recent sample from
**2010**. The authors compute measures of disclosure **breadth** and compare how legitimacy
factors relate to disclosure in 1977 and 2010. Then they use a standard **valuation model** to
compare the link between CSR disclosure and firm value in the two periods.

## Headline results

From the abstract:

- The **breadth** of CSR disclosure **rose significantly**, for both environmental and social
  information.
- **The relationship between legitimacy factors and CSR disclosure did not differ** across the two
  periods. For environmental disclosure, industry membership was less strongly related to
  differences in reporting, but only for the weighted disclosure score.
- CSR disclosure is **not positively valued by investors**, contrary to the newer mainstream
  studies.

## Reality anchor

**None directly.** The explanatory variables are legitimacy and exposure factors (such as size
and industry), not a measure of conduct.

## How it relates to our project

It gives long-run evidence (three decades) that **disclosure tracks exposure and visibility**.
That is the mechanism behind our **salience hypothesis (H3)**: if more visible firms disclose
more and are written about more, any measure built on disclosure or coverage partly measures
visibility. It also sits with the legitimacy side of the "do bad performers say more or less?"
debate; see `synthesis/extended-literature.md`.

## Limitations

The abstract itself lists these:

- **Industrial firms only,** so no generalisation to other industries.
- **US companies only.**
- The disclosure metrics are limited by what the Ernst & Ernst data recorded for each firm.

Our own addition: the 1970s data and the 2010 data are not built the same way, so differences
in breadth may partly reflect how disclosure was recorded.
